# Before you go out

Step outside on a clear night and at first only a few stars show. Wait, and more and more of them appear, though
the sky has not changed at all: your eyes have. One rule comes first: never look at the Sun. Then two things
decide what you will see: how dark the sky is where you stand, and how used to the dark your eyes are. How do you get
ready to see the most, safely?

## The Sun: never by eye

Before any night out, one daytime rule. Never look at the Sun, not even when the Moon covers most of it (NASA's
eclipse safety guidance). Ordinary sunglasses, however dark, are not safe. A solar viewer, or eclipse glasses, made
for looking at the Sun, is thousands of times darker than sunglasses, and should meet the international standard ISO
12312-2. Looking at any part of the bright Sun through a camera lens, binoculars or a telescope causes severe eye
injury at once, unless a special solar filter is fixed over the front. Wearing eclipse glasses does not make it safe
to look through binoculars or a telescope either.

There is a safe way that needs no special glasses: a pinhole projector. Stand with your back to the Sun and let its
light pass through a small hole in one card onto a second card in front of you, and watch the image on the second
card. Never look at the Sun through the pinhole.

## The sky you can see

Light from streets and buildings brightens the whole sky, and the faint stars vanish into it. Astronomers describe a
star's brightness by its magnitude, and a bigger magnitude means a fainter star. The National Park Service's guide
to rating a night sky gives the faintest magnitude you can see: about 2 in a city, 4 in a suburb, 6 in the country,
and 7 under a truly dark sky, far from any lights.

Try it on a sky drawn from a star catalog, on the astrometrics page:

https://astrometrics.cpuchip.net/?thread=measure&beat=ptolemy-magnitudes

It opens with three bright stars lit, Aldebaran, Betelgeuse and Rigel, and draws the sky as a truly dark place shows
it, down to magnitude 7, then dims by itself to a suburb's 4. Drag the slider for the faintest magnitude drawn down to
a city's 2 and watch the fainter stars vanish. Under a truly dark sky you can see stars $7 − 2 = 5$ magnitudes fainter
than in a city. What five magnitudes means in brightness comes later in the course.

## Eyes that open slowly

After being in white light, your eyes take 20 to 30 minutes to adjust to the dark, the National Park Service's
stargazing advice says. Red light disturbs them much less, so observers use a red flashlight, or a white one covered
with red cellophane. One brief flash of white light, from a phone or a car's headlights, can set your night vision
back by several minutes.

So plan the wait. You step outside at 9:40 in the evening and give your eyes 25 minutes. 9:40 is 40 minutes past
9, and 25 more is $40 + 25 = 65$ minutes past 9. An hour has 60 minutes, so 65 minutes past 9 is $65 − 60 = 5$ minutes
past 10: your eyes are ready at 10:05.

## Exercises

1. You go outside at 8:50 and give your eyes 30 minutes. When are they ready?
2. You drive from a suburb to a truly dark sky. How many magnitudes fainter can you see?

## Answers

1. 50 minutes past 8 and 30 more is $50 + 30 = 80$ minutes past 8, and 80 minutes past 8 is $80 − 60 = 20$ minutes
   past 9: ready at 9:20.
2. A suburb shows stars to magnitude 4, a truly dark sky to 7: $7 − 4 = 3$ magnitudes fainter.
